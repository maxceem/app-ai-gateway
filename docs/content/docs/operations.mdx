## Hosted deployment checklist

The hosted deployment's Wrangler profile is gitignored, so the settings that
live in the Cloudflare dashboard rather than in this repository are listed here.
Apply them once per environment and re-check them after any zone change.

**Rate limiting rules on the gateway's zone.** The Worker cannot limit
authentication attempts on its own — its memory is per isolate, so an in-memory
counter, Better Auth's included, sees a fraction of the traffic and limits
nothing. Create three [rate limiting rules](/deployment#rate-limiting),
each counting by client IP:

| Expression | Limit |
| --- | --- |
| `http.request.uri.path eq "/v1/auth/sign-in/email"` | 10 per minute, block for 10 minutes |
| `http.request.uri.path matches "^/v1/auth/(sign-up\|forget-password\|reset-password)"` | 5 per minute |
| `http.request.uri.path matches "^/v1/apps/[^/]+/auth/(challenge\|token\|register)$"` | 60 per minute |

The first bounds password guessing against the console. The second bounds
account creation and password-reset mail. The third covers the unauthenticated
App Attest and token-exchange routes, where a legitimate app makes a handful of
calls per hour per device. If the zone's plan allows only one rule, keep the
sign-in rule; the `matches` operator needs a Business plan, and the deployment
page gives the regex-free form for lower plans.

## The monthly request allowance

The gateway enforces exactly one quota: `maxRequestsPerMonth`, a monthly
allowance shared by every app, key and user you own. Its boundaries follow your
Free-plan anniversary or your subscription schedule; they do not
have to fall on the first day of a UTC calendar month. The allowance comes from
the billing plan (see [Cloud billing](/cloud-billing)) — from its subscription's
plan, or from the billing service's default plan when no subscription grants it
one. A self-hosted deployment, and any plan that does not state an
allowance, is unlimited.

**One request is counted at the moment it is admitted for dispatch to a
provider**, and never before. Everything the gateway can refuse on its own —
a rejected credential, a disallowed model or path, a malformed body, an
unconfigured or disabled provider, an unpriced model, a blocked user, a
caller with no plan — is settled before that point and costs nothing.

Two consequences follow from the boundary being dispatch rather than success:

- A named endpoint that falls through its whole fallback chain still spends one
  request. Retries belong to the incoming request, not to the allowance.
- A request the provider then fails stays counted. It was dispatched; what the
  upstream did with it afterwards is not something the allowance can take back.

Exhaustion answers `429` with code `billing_request_quota_exceeded`, a
`Retry-After` header, and a machine-readable body:

```json
{
  "error": {
    "code": "billing_request_quota_exceeded",
    "message": "The plan's monthly request allowance of 10000 is exhausted until 2026-10-18T09:30:00.000Z",
    "data": {
      "periodId": "paid:2026-05-18T09:30:00.000Z:2026-09-18T09:30:00.000Z",
      "periodStart": "2026-09-18T09:30:00.000Z",
      "periodEnd": "2026-10-18T09:30:00.000Z",
      "limit": 10000,
      "used": 10000,
      "resetAt": "2026-10-18T09:30:00.000Z"
    }
  }
}
```

`resetAt` is the exact start of the next allowance period,
which is when a whole allowance becomes available again. It equals `periodEnd`;
`periodId` is opaque. Refused requests are stored in the usage log with status
`blocked_billing`, so an exhausted period is visible in the console rather than
only in client logs.

The count lives in a single `OrgQuota` Durable Object, and the claim
is a single conditional SQL upsert, so concurrent requests can never push the
period past its allowance.

You do not have to wait for the first `429` to see where the period stands.
`GET /v1/admin/billing/status` returns `quota` — its ID, start, end, count,
the plan's `limit`, and `resetAt` — and the console renders it on the billing
page and raises a banner above every page once four fifths of the allowance is
spent.

On the first deployment of anniversary periods, existing calendar-month
counters remain stored for history but are not mapped into a new schedule. Every
deployment therefore starts with a fresh counter in its resolved anniversary
period. No account, application, provider, credential, or usage-log data
needs to be deleted.

### Not to be confused with an application's own limits

The allowance above is the one quota this gateway imposes on *you*. It is
separate from the per-user and per-app limits you set on your own applications,
which constrain your users rather than you, and are documented in
[Application configuration](/configuration).

Those application limits continue to use UTC calendar months. Their boundaries
do not move with your plan allowance.

Those are checked first, so a request your own limits refused never spends your
allowance. They answer `429 app_rate_limited` or `429 app_budget_exhausted`,
each carrying a `Retry-After` and a `data.scope` of `"user"` or `"app"`, and
they record as `blocked_app_rate` and `blocked_app_budget` in the usage log —
distinct from `blocked_billing` above, so the console can say which quota
stopped a request.

The prefix is the rule: `billing_*` is something your plan decided, `app_*` is
something your own configuration decided.

### When billing cannot be read

The allowance comes from the billing service on every request, so a request that
cannot be checked against it is refused. That refusal is `503
billing_unavailable` with a short `Retry-After`, deliberately not the
`402 billing_payment_required` a caller with no plan at all receives: an
unreachable billing service is not a verdict on anyone's subscription, and a
client that treats it as one shows a paying customer an upsell during your
outage. Treat `402` as something the customer must act on and `503` as a wait.

Note that a lapsed subscription is not a `402`. Where the billing service
configures a default plan, a caller that never subscribed or whose
subscription ended falls back to it and keeps serving traffic against its
allowance; `402` means no plan resolved at all.

### What an unlimited deployment is trading away

This allowance is the gateway's only volume control, so a deployment without
one has no gateway-side ceiling on how much a compromised credential, a looping
client, or a runaway agent can spend at your providers. That is the deliberate
trade for a single, comprehensible quota, and it applies to every self-hosted
deployment by default.

The gateway still bounds *what* those requests may do — an app reaches only the
providers, paths and models it is configured for, only priced models proxy at
all, and an operator can block a user or disable an app or provider instance at
any time — but not *how many* of them there are. Where volume matters, put the
ceiling where the money is: set spend limits and billing alerts in each provider
account, and watch the request and cost trends on the console's **Usage** tab.

## Block an end user

`POST /v1/admin/apps/{app}/users/{user}/block` — the **Block** action on the
console's **Users** tab — writes the flag to the two places that read it: the
`app_user` row, and the user's own coordination object.

The two are read at different moments, so they take effect at different ones:

- **Token exchange is immediate.** `POST /v1/apps/{app}/auth/token` and
  `/auth/register` read `app_user` in D1, so from the moment the call returns a
  blocked user is refused `403 auth_required` and mints no new gateway token.
- **A token already issued stops working within ten seconds.** The request path
  reads the flag through a ten-second per-isolate cache, so it costs no round
  trip on the requests — nearly all of them — that are not blocked. The isolate
  that served the block drops its own cached entry at once; the rest converge
  within the ten seconds.

Unblocking has the same shape, and the same ten-second tail.

## Rotate a provider credential

Rotate in place from the console's **Providers** page — **Update key** in the
row's actions menu — or with `PUT /v1/admin/providers/{id}`. The row keeps its
id, slug, encryption context, and custom pricing; the new credential is
re-encrypted and, like every write, stored unchecked. Warm isolates pick it up within about a minute,
which is also the tail on a deletion.

A gateway-routed instance owns no key of its own, so that call answers
`409 provider_gateway_managed`. Rotate the shared token once with **Update
token** on the gateway's row, or `POST /v1/admin/provider-gateways/{id}/rotate`,
and every instance behind that gateway follows. See [Providers](/providers).

## Reprice stored usage

`POST /v1/admin/apps/{app}/usage/reprice` recomputes stored `cost_usd` for one
provider type, model, and month. Each event is priced through the provider
instance that served it, falling back to the shipped catalog, so two instances of
one type can reprice the same model differently.

Run it as a dry run first (`apply: false`). The preview is tolerant: events whose
serving instance was deleted, and which no catalog price covers, are reported as
`unpriced_events` (with their current spend as `unpriced_cost_usd`) rather than
failing the call. `matched_events` counts only what would actually be rewritten.

`apply: true` is strict — if any matched event cannot be priced the whole call
answers `400 invalid_request` and nothing is written, so a month is never left
half-repriced. Clear the preview's `unpriced_events` first, either by re-adding
the missing custom price or by narrowing the month.

Events billed on a cost the upstream reported (`cost_source: "reported"`) are
never matched: that figure is what you were charged, and a local price would
replace a fact with an estimate.

A repriced event's `cost_source` becomes `computed`, because the local catalog
is now where its cost came from — including events that were previously
[unresolved](#unresolved-usage), whose marker clears once a price can answer for
them.

**Events with no readable usage keep their `cost_source`.** An event whose token
counts are all zero was never metered — an unrecognised response shape, or a
stream a client abandoned before the chunk carrying its usage. Multiplying zero
tokens by any price yields zero, which is arithmetic rather than an answer, so
repricing writes that zero to `cost_usd` but leaves the marker alone: an
`unresolved` event stays `unresolved`. Those events are counted in
`matched_events` (their `cost_usd` really is rewritten) and reported separately
as `unmetered_events`.

A non-zero `unmetered_events` means the month contains spend nothing could
measure. Repricing cannot recover it, and must not appear to have done so —
were the marker overwritten, the events would read as genuinely free, drop out
of the **By cost source** breakdown, and stop being shown as unresolved in the
console, while the unmeasured spend continued.

## Where a cost comes from

Every event records how its `cost_usd` was arrived at, in `cost_source`:

| `cost_source` | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| `reported` | The upstream said what the request cost, and that figure is the billed one. Only [OpenRouter](/providers#openrouter) reports one today; the same number is kept in `reported_cost_usd`. |
| `computed` | Priced locally, from the provider instance's custom pricing or the shipped catalog. |
| `unresolved` | Neither source could answer on a successful response — see below. |

Reported cost wins because it is definitionally what you were charged. Local
pricing is the default and the fallback: if a cost-reporting route ever answers
without a cost, a local price for that model is used, and the event is
`computed` rather than `reported`.

## Unresolved usage

Usage is read from the provider's own response. When a provider answers
successfully but reports usage in a shape this gateway does not know, there is
nothing to price: the request is served, the event is stored with no tokens, and
its cost is zero because nothing was measurable — not because nothing was spent.

Three other situations land in the same place:

- A cost-reporting route answers without a cost, and the model has no local
  price either.
- A model has no local price on a route that does not report cost. Unpriced
  models are refused before they proxy, so this means a price was removed while
  a request was already in flight — within the one-minute configuration cache.
- **A client aborts a streamed response before the final chunk.** Disconnecting
  cancels the provider call, so nothing keeps generating, but the usage a
  provider only sends at the end is lost with it. Such events carry
  `client_aborted: 1` and a `usage_unresolved_cost` line with
  `reason: "client_aborted"`. See the known limitation below.

### Known limitation: aborted streams on cost-reporting routes

On a cost-reporting route the cost arrives in the *last* chunk of a stream. A
client that disconnects before that chunk takes the figure with it, and the
request is recorded `unresolved` at `$0` — the tokens generated up to the
disconnect were charged by [OpenRouter](/providers#openrouter), but nothing
debits your cost reporting. The disconnect cancels the provider call, so the
generation stops there; deliberately repeated, it is still unmeasured spend.

The gateway does not reconcile this today. Doing it properly means looking the
request up through OpenRouter's generation-lookup API after the fact, which is a
funded backlog item rather than something to guess at. Until then:

**The operator signal is a sustained unresolved count on cost-reporting
providers.** Watch the **By cost source** breakdown filtered to those provider
instances, and alert on `usage_unresolved_cost` log lines whose provider is one
of them. A background rate is normal; a rising one from a single app or user is
worth investigating.

What bounds the damage in the meantime depends on the deployment, and it is worth
being exact about it. A hosted deployment on a plan with a
[`maxRequestsPerMonth`](#the-monthly-request-allowance) has a real ceiling: the
month cannot cost more than that many dispatched requests, whatever any of them
turned out to cost. **A self-hosted deployment, and any plan that
states no allowance, has no such ceiling** — that is deliberate, and it is the
trade for having no other quota. There, the bound has to come from outside the
gateway: your own provider-side spend caps and billing alerts at the provider,
and disabling the offending application or provider instance from the console
while you investigate.

Those events are marked `cost_source: "unresolved"` instead of `"computed"`, are
shown as **unresolved** rather than `$0.00` in the console's event list, and can
be counted with the **By cost source** breakdown. Each one also emits an
`error`-level `usage_unresolved_cost` log line naming the app, provider, model,
and route, so a new provider or a changed response format surfaces as an alert
rather than as free traffic. Live requests are never rejected for it.

A sustained count above zero means real spend is escaping cost reporting, and the
gateway cannot fix that on its own: the response shape has to be added to usage
extraction. Blocked requests never reached a provider and carry no
`cost_source`; events recorded before the field existed carry none either.

## Route attribution on usage events

Every event records the route it was actually sent over, which is known before
the request leaves: `provider_gateway_id` and `provider_gateway_type` name the
gateway that carried it and are null on a direct call, and `credential_source`
is `direct` for an instance holding its own key or `byok` when a gateway serves
it from your own key store. It is never inferred from a successful response, so
a null means nothing settled it rather than "probably yours".

`model_author` records who made the model, resolved when the event is recorded
so it can be grouped in SQL. It answers "how much Google-model spend, on any
route" — a question the provider type cannot, once one provider serves several
authors' models. It is an analytics dimension only: allowlists stay per provider
instance and per app, and the request allowance is shared by all your apps.

`served_provider`, `served_model` and `reported_cost_usd` are for upstreams that
report what actually served a request and what it cost. They are populated on
[OpenRouter](/providers#openrouter) instances, where `served_provider` is
the host OpenRouter routed to and `credential_source` is `byok` only when the
response says your own upstream key paid; they are null everywhere else, and a
null means unknown rather than "no". `cost_usd` remains the billed figure either
way.

Breakdowns by **model author**, **gateway**, and **credential source** are in the
console's usage view alongside the existing ones.

## Rotate the vault key

In `local` mode, add `SECRET_VAULT_LOCAL_KEK_V2` as a Worker secret and set
`SECRET_VAULT_LOCAL_KEK_CURRENT_VERSION=2`. Older versions stay accepted for
decryption, so existing blobs keep working and new writes use the new key; there
is no bulk re-encryption script yet, so keep the old version until every
credential has been rotated through the console. In `kms` mode, key rotation is a
cf-kms operation and does not touch this Worker.

Use Wrangler secret commands or stdin so no secret value enters shell history.

## Break-glass operator recovery

`scripts/recover-access.mjs` resets an operator password or promotes an existing
member to owner when nobody can sign in. It refuses to choose a D1 target
implicitly:

```sh
pnpm recover-access -- --email owner@example.com --password-stdin --remote < /secure/password-file
pnpm recover-access -- --email owner@example.com --promote-owner \
  --organization-id <organization-id> --remote
```

Add `--profile <name>` to target a [deployment profile](/deployment#deployment-profiles).
The script shells out to `wrangler d1 execute` and prints neither the password
nor its hash. Validate the procedure with `--local` first; running it against
the remote database is an explicit owner-operated step.

## Database migrations

Change `src/db/schema.ts`, generate a new Drizzle migration, and commit both the
schema and generated migration artifacts. Apply remote migrations through the
`DB` binding so deployments remain independent of a particular database name.

## Logs and traces

The Worker enables Cloudflare logs and sampled traces. Proxy responses also return
`Server-Timing` measurements for authentication, limit evaluation, and provider
time-to-first-byte.

## Health

`GET /v1/healthz` reports a non-secret `vault` field. `"misconfigured"` means the
`SECRET_VAULT_*` configuration is incomplete, contradictory, or invalid, and every
credential-touching request will answer `provider_unavailable`. The structured
`secret_vault_misconfigured` log line names the offending binding without
printing its value. Monitor this field after any secret change.

## Authentication observability

Every rejected request emits one structured `gateway_error` line carrying the
error `code`, its granular `reason`, the status, the path and method, the
application, and the `X-App-Version` the client sent. Nothing else: no request
body, no token, and no other header, because these lines are emitted for
failures whose payload is a credential. Statuses below 500 log at `warn`, 500
and above at `error`.

Every `POST /v1/apps/{app}/auth/token` and `/auth/register` request — successful
or not — also writes one row to `app_auth_event`, with its outcome, reason,
client version and handler latency. Recording happens after the response and can
never fail the request. Challenge issuance is not recorded: it decides nothing.

### Claim propagation

When an issuer token verifies but a required entitlement claim has not arrived
yet, the gateway opens a window on that user: `app_user.claim_pending_since` is
set on the *first* such refusal and left alone by every retry. The exchange that
finally succeeds computes the wait, stores it as `claim_delay_ms` on its own auth
event, logs `claim_propagation_recovered`, and closes the window.

Two questions follow directly from that:

- Who is stuck activating right now — `app_user.claim_pending_since IS NOT NULL`.
- How long activation normally takes — the p50 and p95 of `claim_delay_ms`.

Both, along with the token-exchange success rate and a by-cause breakdown of
failures, are on the **Auth & Errors** tab of each application in the console,
backed by `GET /v1/admin/apps/{app}/auth-events/summary`.

### Retention

A daily cron trigger deletes `app_auth_event` rows older than 90 days, and
`app_auth_challenge` rows whose five-minute window has already passed. An expired
challenge can no longer be redeemed, so removing it changes nothing a client can
observe; issuing a challenge also runs the same sweep roughly once every hundred
requests, which keeps the table small on a deployment where no cron runs. The
cron touches nothing else — `app_usage_event` is accounting history and is never
pruned.
